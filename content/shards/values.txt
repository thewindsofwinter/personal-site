A Primer on Values
~ 11-09-26 v0

> Abstract: I'm trying to understand why I find certain ideas "elegant" or "inelegant", or startup ideas 
> "appealing" or "unappealing". In the end, I think the source of this taste comes from the values we want
> to promote in our society. Outside the things common to all startups (the sense of accomplishment from 
> creating something from nothing, monetary reward, etc.) I think the joy of creation comes from working
> to promote and sustain values conducive to good living and prosperity: things such as rugged individualism,
> optimism, pragmatism, and equality of opportunity. Two large forces serve to muddle this currently: the
> impending implementation of AGI (a civilizational-scale force that feels inevitable to fall in some form)
> and the accelerating culture of "get rich fast" in startups. This piece attempts to consider what
> technologies advance our current value system mostly in isolation from these themes as a starting point.

There is a belief in various academic circles that the idea of the “American Dream”— that is, the achievement 
of success through hard work and your own merit—exists primarily due to the frontier nation of our country[^1]. 
Citizens of other nations fled pre-existing class structures and oppression, finding a place in the United 
States due to the lack of an entrenched aristocracy or class system. The vast frontier of fertile land and 
natural resources allowed immigrants to imagine instantly achieving their fortune, for example in the 
California Gold Rush.

A key challenge in the present day is the “disappearing frontier.” The end of the Space Race, as well as the 
completion of American territorial expansion, resulted additionally in a closing of the “frontier of the mind” 
or “frontiers of thought”. This can be seen in the stagnation of nuclear power, and decrease in transformative 
innovation outside the information technology sector[^2]. In the period 1900-2000, the world underwent 
order-of-magnitude changes in communications, mass transit, health, agricultural efficiency, and manufactured 
goods. Outside of computing technology, the world has made no fundamental advances since then in the period 
2000-2025. We have moved from a regime of “growing” a pie of raw materials and energy to squeezing out crumbs 
by efficiently allocating the same. Articulating a coherent vision for optimism in the next century is 
therefore of critical importance for the innovator.

My grandparents witnessed over their lifetime an increase of literacy from near-zero to one hundred percent, 
the eradication of preventable disease, and an increase in wealth a thousandfold (from peasantry on the brink 
of starvation to a comfortable city life), not to mention modern conveniences like air conditioning, plumbing, 
and television. When I was eleven, I went to the ancestral farm of my family and got a brief taste of the 
progress of the past hundred years. *What can we promise our children?*

Below I briefly attempt to describe the difference between “progressive” and “regressive” technology. The 
former emphasize contact with reality, strengthen American values[^3], and elevate the human spirit. The latter 
encourages retreat from reality, erode American values, and isolate and crush the human spirit. 

I begin from the economic writings of Vaclav Smil and Yi Wen. Smil[^4] describes energy per person as a key 
growth factor for improving the human condition. Technologies can either elevate this (e.g. further energy 
generation, fusion power) or attempt to ration it (e.g. moving to renewables). Similarly, Yi Wen[^5] describes a 
“New Stage Theory” of industrialization progressing from proto-industry to light industry, heavy industry, 
and the welfare state. In each stage, the savings from the previous stage can either be consumed by the 
people or reinvested into the next stage. Premature consumption results in a weak state, exemplified by 
the Greek pension debt crisis and perhaps also the imminent debt crisis in my home state of Illinois. 

Consumption versus investment, redistribution versus growth—these are the key elements in my mind that 
distinguish “regressive” versus “progressive” technology. This occurs at both the individual and societal 
scale. For example: in the youth of our nation, more than one in ten know of a friend in a relationship with 
an AI. Even at Stanford, we question the value of intellectual study in the face of a nebulous AGI. On the 
other hand, AGI promises to grow the pie even further—the victory of humanity over disease, an era of 
abundance. Nowadays, startup culture takes the form of a “land grab” or “fear of missing out”—we will grab 
the money now, since we won’t be able to in the future and get rich quick. But will money mean anything in 
the face of AGI? All is committed to the construction of this egovore whose good or evil is unquestioned. 
The questions of the future will be relationships between humans, who gets to wield the hand of God (note 
the inherent redistributive logic here).

I am not yet sure whether AGI is progressive or regressive technology, although you can clearly see 
progressive and regressive elements within its development.

Let’s talk about things that I find more clearly progressive or regressive. VR and social media are fairly 
clear examples of regressive technology, encouraging isolation and distance from reality. Space exploration—
the development of the Moon, Mars, and near-Earth asteroids—seems clearly a progressive technology and the 
re-establishment of a frontier we should chase after. I believe the “strenuous life” as described by 
Roosevelt is a key factor in producing the spirit of independence, opportunity, optimism, and pragmatism 
characteristic of the American Dream. In its absence, I fear the degeneration of our values to something 
unrecognizable today.

I conclude with some critical technologies that may be progressive or regressive in implementation—the 
development of these may be the greatest challenge of our time. The problem of AGI goes without saying. 
Additionally, the development of cryogenics and longevity pose interesting questions—if development of 
certain technologies (especially space travel) takes centuries, what incentives will the ability to “skip” 
portions of it produce? Will there be children of the future?

Related to this are advances in brain-computer interfaces and transhumanism. It appears clear that the 
ability to think faster and more deeply than currently possible due to our biology is a key capability 
to maintain even a sense of “personhood” and independence in an era of AGI. Possibilities include a 
“singularity” (which is contrary to American values[^7]), a society of superhuman agents, or some human-AI 
hybrid system. 

Finally there is the question of automation, including in manufacturing. On the one hand, this promises 
the ability to make things faster and better from initial idea. On the other, this displaces lots of labor, 
and I wonder whether a society where every person is a one-man corporation is a society that we would like 
to promote. However, I believe large-scale automation of manufacturing, especially of the “new industrial 
trinity” will be essential. This includes next-generation energy sources (thorium fission, fusion, 
large-scale solar / upper-atmosphere and space-based solar, efficient batteries and rocket fuel), 
transportation (e.g. spacecraft, space elevators), and infrastructure (e.g. ecological cycling, 
radiation shielding, agricultural and medical advancements). 

This is far from a detailed manifesto[^6]. But the question “what prosperity and values can we promise our 
grandchildren” is of critical importance to consider. This is a call for reflection: in our hearts, there 
needs to be an *AI 2047* or *Machines of Loving Grace* specifically geared towards propagating our American 
values: liberty, opportunity, optimism, and pragmatic enterprise.

My belief is that a full "singularity" where all minds are part of one mass is bad, primarily due to the fact that evolutionarily we collapse the pool of options available to us to one (for the singularity entity), resulting in adverse fitness effects. From a more historical standpoint, I find this vision of the "singularity" to be akin to the inefficiencies generated by a monopoly, something inimical to American values. A singularity is essentially a monopoly on human thought.
