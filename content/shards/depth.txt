Seeing Deeper
~ 08-29-26 v2 [Ayush]

> We have to learn that these are the kinds of disciplines in the field of science that you have to learn – to know when you know and when you don't know, and what it is you know and what it is you don't know.  You've go to be very careful not to confuse yourself.
>- Richard Feynman ({https://calteches.library.caltech.edu/35/2/PointofView.htm})

What traits do almost all good researchers have? I've noticed that many researchers I admire have a common level of 
meticulousness. These researchers are smart, but what makes them great is the care with which they
do their work. The opposite tail of the distribution would probably be conspiracy theorists. While 
conspiracy theorists lean into the comforting myths that our pattern-matching brains generate, 
true scientists are on guard against surface-level illusions. To look deeper into the well of truth 
we must first overcome the lies we tell ourselves.

A scientific meticulousness goes beyond curiosity. A toddler is curious when they ask why the sky
is blue, but they have no intuition on what would make an answer to this question satisfying. To be 
maximally scientific, we should understand 
the phenomenon down to fundamental laws we accept to be true or fundamental measurements from experiment.
We must avoid the temptation of colloquial explanations: these maintain the semblance of truth,
but fall apart after rigorous experimentation.

A good example of this in robotics, my field of study, are questions related to behavior cloning.
Recently, Seohong wrote a good summary of some of these questions in the following blog post: 
{https://seohong.me/blog/behavioral-cloning-mystery/}. Every problem mentioned here is well-known,
but often hand-waved explanations have been offered which require further scrutiny. For example,
history-conditioned policies are suggested to be hard because of causal confusion, a phenomenon 
where a model overfits to spurious correlations. However, this is not the case in diffusion 
policies, which exhibit a separate problem which I resolve in a previous paper (Torne et al. 2025).
Even after doing such research, I am still astonished at how many of these paradoxes that 
Seohong mentions which I simply waved away.

I consider the researchers above "principled" or theoretical researchers. While many great scientists 
don't fit this mold, I think the meticulousness mentioned above is a fundamental component of good 
research of any style. Indeed, I propose that the key distinguishing factor of different research
styles is where the researcher chooses to apply their meticulousness.

For example, more "empirical" or practical/systems researchers often 
apply no less care in understanding normalization, action spaces, and various 
foundational components of the robot policy. The scale of the practical system 
needed to accomplish such state-of-the-art tasks might not admit the clean isolation of variables
a more theoretically inclined researcher might prefer. Nevertheless, a good practical 
researcher will still carefully hone in on the factors most likely to affect performance, 
and design their policy in a principled manner.

Can we get closer to this ideal of clarity? While genius is uncontrollable, cultivating this 
meticulousness of mind is something which can be improved through practice, much like the other 
practical aspects of excellence*. We should cultivate this virtue and work with others who value 
such insight, to see one level deeper than before.

*For an article aligned with my thoughts on excellence, see <The Mundanity of Excellence> {https://www.jstor.org/stable/202063}
